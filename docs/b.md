The OUP legal history source is very clear: the person/thing distinction that shaped later Western law has its roots in Roman law; Gaius classifies law into persons, things, and actions; and the Roman “law of persons” organizes society through statuses of freedom/slavery, citizenship, and family position. Slaves could be human beings and yet lack the kind of legal standing approximated by *caput*. This is an enormous step in the genealogy of agency because it answers, in a formal way, the question: **who can count in law as the bearer of acts, rights, and obligations?** [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/35026/chapter/298855110)

Then comes the theological turn, and this is where “person” acquires a much deeper metaphysical charge. Early Christian debate uses **persona/hypostasis** to think Trinity and Christology, and Boethius gives the classic formula of person as **“individual substance of rational nature,”** which later medieval thought inherits. SEP’s Aquinas entry notes directly that Thomas accepts Boethius’s definition, though he complicates it in light of his hylomorphic metaphysics. This is one of the biggest steps toward the modern concept, because it fuses metaphysical individuality with rationality in a single term: a person is no longer just a role or legal status, but a being of a certain ontological kind. [OUP Academic+2Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+2](https://academic.oup.com/book/35026/chapter/298855110)

But the even more consequential transformation is **Paulinian and Augustinian inwardness**. Paul is pivotal because conscience becomes an **inner witness**: the law is not only external command but something borne inwardly, written in the heart and registered by conscience. The SEP conscience entry explicitly identifies Paul as central to the Christian understanding of conscience as inward-looking witness. Augustine then radicalizes this. His moral psychology shifts responsibility toward **inner consent**, and SEP notes that he comes closer than any earlier philosopher to positing will as a faculty of choice irreducible to reason or non-rational desire. More than that, Augustine makes the decisive move by locating moral responsibility in what is inwardly assented to, not merely what outwardly occurs. This creates the great Western chamber of guilt, confession, motive, and self-scrutiny: a person becomes someone answerable not only for deeds but for the orientation of will and love. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2021/entries/conscience/)

At this point, the trajectory toward modernity is visible. **Locke** reframes the person as a self-reflective being that can regard itself as the same across time, and he calls “person” a **forensic term** appropriating actions and their merit. That formulation is extremely important: personhood is explicitly tied to accountability, reward, punishment, and continuity of consciousness. **Kant** then performs the most powerful synthesis in the Western tradition. On the theoretical side, the “I think” must be able to accompany all my representations; on the moral side, the rational being is an **end in itself** with dignity because of rational autonomy. Here the metaphysical bearer, the legal-accountable person, and the inward moral agent are fused into one structure: the **autonomous rational subject-person**. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+4Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+4Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-personal-identity/)

After Kant, that structure is first **socialized** and then **deconstructed**. Hegel argues that self-consciousness is not self-grounding; it arises through **mutual recognition**. So personhood is not just inward possession but social achievement within a normative order. Foucault then historicizes the whole formation: the modern subject is not merely the source of action and knowledge but also the product of power, discipline, and biopolitical administration. His account of biopower is exactly the point at which the genealogy of the person turns into the genealogy of managed life: power no longer only kills or commands; it administers, optimizes, regulates, and normalizes. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2023/entries/recognition/)

So the deepest synthesis I can offer is this:

The modern Western “Person” is assembled from **five different inventions**:

1.  **relational personhood**: the person as a node in kinship, ritual, exchange, and office;

2.  **epic singularization**: the person as a named, narratable, recognizable individual;

3.  **juridical personhood**: the person as status-bearing locus of rights, duties, and imputability;

4.  **interiorized moral personhood**: the person as conscience, will, and answerable inner life before God;

5.  **autonomous/transcendental subjectivity**: the person as self-legislating rational bearer of experience and dignity. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+6National Academic OER & Digital Library+6faculty.uml.edu+6](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/53454/1/58.Chris%20Fowler.pdf)


On that view, **Enkidu** is not yet a “person” in the modern sense; he is a drama of **becoming socially human**. **Odysseus** is not yet a Kantian subject; he is a drama of **identity through narrative, recognition, and strategic self-possession**. **Rome** makes that identity legally count. **Paul and Augustine** make it inwardly guilty. **Boethius and Aquinas** make it metaphysically definable. **Locke and Kant** make it reflexive and universal. **Hegel** makes it social. **Foucault** shows how all of it can be turned back into administration. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+8Yale University Press+8Oxford University Research Archive+8](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2020/04/30/the-epic-of-gilgamesh/)

The most important takeaway is this: **the Person was not discovered; it was stratified.** The oldest strata are not inward autonomy but **relation, office, name, and ritual role**. The modern person only appears when **epic singularity, Roman imputability, Christian inwardness, and philosophical universality** collapse into one figure. That is why your instinct is right: to do serious research here, one has to study not one history but several histories that eventually fused. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+3National Academic OER & Digital Library+3OUP Academic+3](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/53454/1/58.Chris%20Fowler.pdf)

For an actual deep research program, I would split the archive into five dossiers: **epic individuation** (*Gilgamesh*, *Odyssey*), **royal-sacral mediation** (Egypt, Israel, Assmann), **Roman legal personality**, **Christian conscience/will** (Paul, Augustine, Boethius), and **modern subjectivity** (Locke, Kant, Hegel, Foucault). That avoids the main mistake in this field, which is treating “person,” “self,” “subject,” “agent,” and “legal individual” as if they were the same thing from the start.
