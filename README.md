# The Invention of the Person

A Genealogy of Stratified Personhood.

Contemporary political and moral thought treats the Person, the autonomous, rational, rights-bearing individual, as a natural category. We treat it as a stratification: a composite assembled over millennia from at least five historical formations, each built by specific institutions to answer a specific problem. Relational personhood, rooted in kinship, ritual, and animism, settles who may be recognized. Epic singularization in *Gilgamesh* and the *Odyssey* makes a single life narratable. Roman law defines the *persona* as a bearer of rights and liabilities. Paul, Augustine, and Boethius add an interior conscience answerable before God and a metaphysical definition of the person. Locke and Kant consolidate the autonomous rational subject that generates moral law from within. The modern individual appears when these layers are fused and retroactively treated as one thing; Hegel's theory of recognition socializes the figure and Foucault's genealogy historicizes it. Contemporary platform capitalism, algorithmic management, the extension of legal personhood to non-human entities, and neurotechnology thin the practical conditions of agency while leaving formal personhood intact. Three small models make parts of the argument auditable. Dating the nine formations reproduces the narrative order over a literate span of 4,075 years. A geometric-mean index with formal support fixed at 1.00 gives a profiled human a practical index of 0.39 and an infrastructural human 0.93. In a recognition network, concentrating recognition on six of 120 agents leaves profiled agents with 0.36 reciprocated ties against 9.5 for the minority. Personhood, having been assembled, can be disassembled; today the disassembly proceeds by hollowing institutional supports while the legal category stays in place.
